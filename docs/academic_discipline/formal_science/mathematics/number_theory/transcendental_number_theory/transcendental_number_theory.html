概要

 

リウヴィルの定理

 超越数の理論は、1844年のリウヴィルによる有名な論文に端を発している。この論文タイトルでも説明されているように、彼は、整数係数の代数方程式を満たさない数という「非常に広い（très-étendue）」クラスを初めて構築した。しかし、この主題に関連するいくつかの個別的な問題は、この日付よりはるか以前にすでに提起されており、関連する無理数の研究は、それに先立つ少なくとも1世紀間、主要な注目の対象となっていた。

 実際、1744年にはオイラーがすでに $e$ の無理性を確立し、1761年にはランベルトが $\pi$ の無理性を確認していた。さらに、連分数の初期研究において、無理数を有理数で近似する際のいくつかの基本的な特徴が明らかにされていました。たとえば、任意の無理数 $\alpha$ に対して、有理数 $p/q \ (q \gt 0)$ の無限列が存在し、$|\alpha - p/q| \lt 1/q^{2}$ が成立することが知られていました。また、二次無理数の連分数は最終的に周期的であるため、ある定数 $c = c(\alpha) > 0$ が存在し、すべての有理数 $p/q \ (q \gt 0)$ に対して $|\alpha - p/q| \gt c/q^{2}$ が成立することも知られていました。

 Some isolated problems pertaining to the subject, however, had been formulated long before this date, and the closely related study of irrational numbers had constituted a major focus of attention for at least a century preceding. Indeed, by 1744, Euler had already established the irrationality of $e$, and, by 1761, Lambert had confirmed the irrationality of $\pi$. Moreover, the early studies of continued fractions had revealed several basic features concerning the approximation of irrational numbers by rationals. It was known, for instance, that for any irrational $\alpha$ there exists an infinite sequence of rationals $p/q (q \gt 0)$ such thatt $|\alpha - p/q| \lt 1/q^{2}$, and it was known also that the continued fraction of a quadratic irrational is ultimately periodic, whence there exists $c = c(\alpha) > 0$ such that $|\alpha - p/q| \gt c/q^{2}$ for all rationals $p/q (q \gt 0)$. Liouville observed that a result of the latter kind holds more generally, and that there exists in fact a limit to the accuracy with which any algebraic number, not itself rational, can be approximated by rationals. It was this observation that provided the first practical criterion whereby transcendental numbers could be constructed.

Theorem 1.1. For any algebraic number a with degree $n \gt 1$, there exists $c = c(\alpha) > 0$ such that $|\alpha - p/q| \gt c/q^{2}$ for all rationals $p/q (q \gt 0)$. The theorem follows almost at once from the definition of an algebraic number. A real or complex number is said to be algebraic if it is a zero of a polynomial with integer coefficients; every algebraic number $\alpha$ is the zero of some such irreducible polynomial, say $P$, unique up to a constant multiple, and the degree of $\alpha$ is defined as the degree of $P$. It suffices to prove the theorem when $\alpha$ is real; in this case, for any rational $p/q (q \gt 0)$, we have by the mean value theorem: \[ -P (p/q) = P(\alpha) - P(p/q) = (\alpha - p/q) P'(\xi) \] for some $\xi$ between $p/q$ and $\alpha$. Clearly one can assume that $|\alpha - p/q| \lt 1$, for the result would otherwise be valid trivially; then $|\xi| \lt 1 + |\alpha|$ and thus $|P'(\xi)| \lt 1/c$ for some $c = c(\alpha) > 0$; hence \[ |\alpha - p/q| \gt c|P(p/q)|. \] But, since $P$ is irreducible, we have $P(p/q) \neq 0$, and the integer $|q^{n}P(p/q)|$ is therefore at least $1$; the theorem follows. Note that one can easily give an explicit value for $c$; in fact one can take \[ c^{-1} = n^{2}(1 + |\alpha|)^{n-1}H, \] where $H$ denotes the height of $\alpha$, that is, the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients of $P$.
